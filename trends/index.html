Where the field is heading
1

Adoption

"Will we use AI?" — answered: yes, 87%
›

The access question is settled. 87% of L&D teams use AI in some form — but only 12% of individual employees use it daily. The gap isn't awareness. It's the absence of structured, repeated practice embedded in actual work.

The Booking.com case defined the shift: they replaced "access" with a measurable goal (12+ active days/month), supported by 2-day workshops and recurring office hours. Adoption jumped 65%. Duolingo went further: removing environment-setup friction via Codespaces had more impact than any training program.

2

Governance

"How do we govern it?"
›

Microsoft's $2.5B "forward-deployed" model settled the governance debate: governance isn't an IT policy — it's a culture-building program. Embedding engineers directly in workflows, not training them separately, is the model that works.

Go1 found 80% of IT leaders discovered AI tools deployed without review. The governance failure isn't policy gaps — it's L&D teams treating governance as someone else's problem. iVentiv's analysis: orchestrate across culture, legal, HR, leadership — not just IT.

3

Measurement

"How do we prove it works?"
›

Less than a quarter of L&D teams measure whether training achieves organizational goals (ATD 2026). 83% of vendors sell analytics as feature #1 — the product moved faster than the practice.

Faros AI's finding is the tell: AI adoption increased task volume 21%, but PR review time grew 91%. Measuring output alone missed the bottleneck entirely. The behavioral signal — not self-report, not completion rate — is the only measurement that holds.

4

Reshape

"How do we redesign work around it?"
›

BCG's 14,000-worker survey: 72% say AI has already changed their skills expectations. 42% are expected to figure it out alone (Bright Horizons). Amazon targeted 7× upskilling for 700,000 employees — not tool access, role reinvention.

BCG data shows 25 percentage points higher business impact when AI strategy accompanies tools versus tools alone. The unit of change is the team workflow, not the individual tool license.

AI-Native NOW

"Is our infrastructure built for this, or just bolt-on?"
›

Workday Learning + Sana went GA July 22, 2026 — the first Tier 1 LMS built AI-first. 3× learner engagement, 98% faster content creation, learning paths tied to real HCM data. The evaluation criterion has shifted: not "does it have AI features?" but "is its AI foundational or cosmetic?"

TalentLMS found 59% of employees use AI for tasks they were never trained for — invisible in any standard LMS report. That's a measurement gap AND an infrastructure gap: most learning systems weren't designed to detect this.

1
Adoption
Moving from access to real, repeated use
12%
of employees use AI daily — despite 87% org-level adoption. The gap is structured practice, not awareness. (CLO / Gallup ↗)

The Booking.com case is the clearest playbook available: replace "we gave them access" with a measurable use target (they chose 12+ active days/month), then back it with 2-day hands-on workshops and recurring weekly office hours. Adoption jumped 65%. The structural lesson: access is a precondition, not an outcome. (DX / Booking.com case ↗)

Remove setup friction before anything else. Duolingo's internal experience: eliminating environment-setup friction via GitHub Codespaces had more measurable impact on AI adoption than any training they ran. Developers who spend their first day fighting setup don't adopt. (GitHub / Duolingo ↗)

Don't stop at literacy (safe baseline use). Most 2026 programs stop at literacy — the wrong finish line. The target is fluency: using AI for real advantage, knowing when not to trust it, developing judgment about what to hand off. Stripe's onboarding model — real backlog tasks with a mentor, not toy projects — produces developers who are fluent, not just licensed. (Stripe Atlas ↗)

🍋 At Lemonade

Your 3+ month onboarding ramp is the adoption window — for engineers and PMs alike. Don't use it for content delivery — use it for structured practice with real tasks from the actual backlog. Set a measurable use target (e.g., AI-assisted on at least 3 real tasks in the first 4 weeks — PRs for engineers, specs or discovery for product). Add a retrieval layer over existing Slack threads, runbooks, PR history, and roadmap docs — make existing knowledge findable before adding new content. Senior engineers and experienced PMs are your highest-resistance cohorts; target them with workflow-level enablement, not general AI literacy training.

Governance
Building judgment into the workflow, not a compliance layer on top
80%
of IT leaders discovered AI tools deployed without IT review — shadow AI is already the norm, not the exception. (Go1 survey ↗)

Microsoft's $2.5B "forward-deployed" AI implementation model defines the current benchmark: embed engineers directly into team workflows instead of training them separately. The governance insight isn't about policy — it's that governance and enablement are the same program, not sequential steps. (CNBC ↗)

Build "when to trust AI / when to challenge it" directly into existing artifacts — PR templates, code review guides, incident post-mortems. Not a course, not a policy doc. The judgment habit becomes governance when it lives in the work itself. The Wharton finding is the risk case: people accepted wrong AI answers 80% of the time, confidently. The confident tone is the hazard. (iVentiv ↗)

On vendor evaluation: only 24% of AI features promised in vendor roadmap slides are actually live (Fosway Group 2026). Require a running demo, not a slide deck. Ask specifically: is the AI connected to our actual employee data, or is it generating from a generic model with no organizational context?

🍋 At Lemonade

Start with a 5-question AI tool census — what tools are actually in use across engineering and product right now, licensed or not. That's your real curriculum. Then build "why do you trust this?" into both PR templates and product spec templates as a single required field — cheap, structural, and it builds the evaluation habit without a training module. Don't delegate AI governance to IT. Orchestrate it across engineering culture, product team leads, and onboarding — that's where the actual decisions happen.

The ATD 2026 State of the Industry report: less than a quarter of L&D teams measure whether training achieves organizational goals. Completion rates are still the dominant metric. Meanwhile 83% of vendors sell analytics as their #1 feature — the product has moved faster than the practice. (ATD ↗)

The Faros AI finding is the measurement case study: teams measured AI adoption by task volume (up 21%) and called it a win. The actual outcome — PR review time up 91% — only appeared when they measured cycle time and bottleneck location. The signal you need is behavioral and real-time, not a quarterly survey. (Faros AI ↗)

Schneider Electric's approach: replace skills surveys entirely with GitHub behavioral signals — commits, code review patterns, PR frequency — as a living skills atlas. IBM/Eightfold AI did the same for internal mobility. Actual work-sample data > self-report, every time. (Schneider Electric ↗, 100-dev study ↗)

🍋 At Lemonade

Engineers: add "explain the core logic of your most significant change this week" to the code review template. PMs: add "what was the most important assumption you tested this week and what changed?" to retros or 1:1 templates. Both fields surface learning debt before it compounds — zero cost to implement. Define behavioral change metrics at week 1, 4, and 12 before designing any program — not the training content, the behavioral target. For engineers, GitHub activity data (PR frequency, review patterns, commit patterns by cohort) is your skills atlas. For PMs, roadmap velocity and spec iteration cycles are equivalent signals. Both already exist; you just need to read them.

Reshape
Team-level workflow redesign, not individual tool access
+25pp
higher business impact when AI strategy accompanies tools — versus tools alone. The unit of change is the team workflow, not the individual license. (BCG, 14K workers ↗)

BCG's survey of 14,000 workers finds that 72% say AI has already changed their skills expectations, and 50-55% of US jobs will be substantially changed in the next 2-3 years. But 42% of employees are expected to figure it out alone (Bright Horizons). Amazon's Future Ready program overshot its 7× upskilling target for 700,000 employees — by designing for role reinvention, not tool access. (Bright Horizons ↗)

The Faros AI finding tells you where individual adoption breaks down: task volume increases, but PR review time grows 91% because the constraint shifts to senior engineers. Individual AI access without workflow redesign just moves the bottleneck upstream. Build an explicit review triage standard — which AI-generated code gets fast-tracked vs. deep review — as a teachable, transferable skill embedded in onboarding. (Faros AI ↗)

Start with one squad, not a company-wide program. Map where AI is being used and where the human judgment bottleneck is in that squad's specific workflow. Redesign that single step. Then scale the pattern. BCG's data: teams with a clear AI strategy see 25pp more business impact than those with just tool access.

🍋 At Lemonade

Pick one squad — engineering or product. Run a 2-week observation: where is AI being used, and where does work pile up (a senior engineer's review queue, or a PM's discovery-to-spec handoff)? That bottleneck is your first design problem. Build a 3-item AI practice minimum into tech onboarding for both roles — one prompt pattern to use, one anti-pattern to avoid, one review or validation habit to apply. Not a course, a standard. Internal mobility at Lemonade will accelerate when you remove the first blocker (tenure/manager approval gates) before adding the tool layer. Design mobility as friction removal first, enablement second.
